Sentiment Analysis with LLMs

This project is an update of the earlier Letterboxd Sentiment work. The original version focused on scraping Letterboxd movie reviews and analyzing them with a classical sentiment pipeline based on VADER and word-frequency visualizations. After changes in site protections made large-scale scraping unreliable, the project was rethought rather than abandoned. The new version keeps the original intuition of comparing audience reactions through review text, but upgrades the data source and the analysis layer: instead of relying only on a single scalar sentiment score, it now combines official Steam user reviews with LLM-based multidimensional interpretation.

In practice, this turns the project into both a continuation and an expansion. The first iteration remains a useful case study in classical sentiment analysis over movie reviews, while the updated version moves toward a richer workflow in which language models can separate different kinds of reactions such as enjoyment, frustration, immersion, value satisfaction, and technical satisfaction. The result is not just a positive vs. negative split, but a more structured picture of how users talk about a title.



01 - History of Sentiment Analysis

Modern sentiment analysis is usually associated with the early 2000s, when the growth of online reviews, forums, and product feedback created a strong demand for automated ways to measure opinion at scale. Yet the roots of the field are older. Early work relied on hand-built lexicons: researchers assembled lists of words associated with positive or negative affect and then counted their occurrences inside a document. Even this simple approach already suggested an important idea that still survives today: language carries measurable emotional signals, and those signals can be aggregated into something quantitatively meaningful.

A second stage came with machine learning approaches that treated sentiment as a text classification problem. Instead of relying only on manually curated vocabularies, models learned from labeled examples. Bag-of-words, n-grams, TF-IDF features, and linear classifiers became standard tools because they were lightweight and often surprisingly effective. For review data in particular, these methods established a practical baseline: sentiment could be modeled as a function of lexical choice, repetition, and emphasis, even when the underlying semantics were not deeply understood.

The current stage goes further. Transformer models and LLMs make it possible to move beyond a single polarity score and interpret reviews in a more contextual way. A modern system can still preserve the strengths of classical sentiment analysis , reproducibility, fast scoring, distribution plots, interpretable word clouds , while adding a higher-level layer that extracts multiple dimensions of user perception. That is exactly the perspective adopted in this upgraded project.



02 - Database: Letterboxd Movie Reviews

The original project undertook a sentiment analysis to compare the audience's emotional responses toward movie directors. An analysis of movie reviews made by users at Letterboxd was carried out, aiming to explore how the sentiments differ for directors in different genres and levels of popularity. That first version is still an important part of the story because it motivated the broader question behind the project: what can review text reveal about the way people emotionally receive a film? It also provided a concrete setting in which sentiment analysis could be tested against recognizable cultural objects rather than abstract benchmark examples. A review attached to a film carries context, expectations, genre conventions, and the influence of a director's previous work, so the text is not just positive or negative in the abstract. It is part of a broader reception process. That made Letterboxd especially interesting as a starting point: users were reacting to identifiable titles in a public and highly opinionated environment, which made it possible to connect linguistic patterns back to a clear source.

We started by scraping data from Letterboxd using BeautifulSoup. We collected many pages of reviews for a given movie and used NLTK's SentimentIntensityAnalyzer to evaluate a sentiment score for each review. This produced a compound metric that allowed the text to be organized into positive, negative, or neutral groups.

By aggregating these scores, it became possible to gauge the overall audience reception of a film and compare it with factors such as genre, popularity, or critical reputation. Excluding zero scores gave a distribution that often looked approximately gaussian. For Interstellar, for example, the resulting curve had a positive center with a moderate spread, suggesting that the audience was not just favorable overall but also clustered around a fairly coherent reaction.

The project then moved beyond a single score by plotting word clouds for positive and negative reviews. The word cloud displays the most frequently occurring words within each sentiment category, with the size of each word indicative of its prevalence in the reviews. This offered a fast qualitative view of the themes audiences associated with a viewing experience and highlighted which descriptors appeared repeatedly on each side of the sentiment divide.

Although a number of words repeated across both positive and negative reviews, requiring further filtering, it was still possible to distinguish certain terms predominantly associated with favorable reactions and others more common in critical reviews. That distinction helped show both the usefulness and the limitations of the purely classical setup: it was already capable of surfacing structure in the data, but there was clearly room for a more nuanced interpretation of what people actually meant when they liked or disliked a work.



03 - The upgrade: LLMs and Steam user reviews

The updated version keeps the same general objective , extracting audience reaction from review text , but changes both the data source and the analytical layer. Because large-scale scraping of Letterboxd reviews became impractical, the project pivoted to official Steam user reviews. This makes the data collection step much more stable while preserving a core advantage of the original project: the text still comes from real user reactions tied to a specific title.

The methodological upgrade is even more important. Instead of relying only on a one-dimensional sentiment polarity score, the new system adds an LLM-based interpretation layer that evaluates multiple dimensions of the review set at once. In the current version, those dimensions are enjoyment, frustration, immersion, value satisfaction, and technical satisfaction. This makes the analysis more expressive: two titles with similarly positive average sentiment can still differ strongly in whether users praise atmosphere, criticize performance, or debate whether the price feels justified.

On the other, the radar chart adds a higher-level multidimensional reading generated from the corpus of reviews. In this sense, the project evolves from classical sentiment analysis into an LLM-augmented review intelligence pipeline: still grounded in interpretable statistics, but no longer limited to a single scalar notion of positive or negative reception.

This upgrade also changes the broader meaning of the project. What began as a movie-review sentiment analyzer now becomes a small framework for comparing how classical NLP and LLM-based interpretation complement one another. The earlier Letterboxd stage remains valuable as a historical and methodological foundation; the Steam and LLM stage pushes the same idea into a more robust, multidimensional, and current direction.
